<h3>Understanding Michigan's No-Fault System</h3>

<p>Michigan's no-fault auto insurance system is unique and often confusing. After an accident, you typically file a claim with your own insurance company for Personal Injury Protection (PIP) benefits—regardless of who caused the crash. These benefits can cover medical expenses, lost wages, and other costs.</p>

<p>However, the 2019-2020 no-fault reforms changed the landscape significantly. Depending on your coverage level, your PIP medical benefits may be capped at $50,000, $250,000, $500,000, or remain unlimited. Understanding your coverage before an accident occurs is critical.</p>

<h3>When Can You Sue the At-Fault Driver?</h3>

<p>Michigan law allows you to pursue a third-party lawsuit against the negligent driver if your injuries meet the "serious impairment of body function" threshold. This requires showing an objectively manifested impairment of an important body function that affects your general ability to lead your normal life.</p>

<p>Successfully meeting this threshold allows you to recover compensation for pain and suffering, excess medical expenses, and other damages not covered by no-fault benefits. Our attorneys have extensive experience proving threshold injuries and maximizing third-party recoveries.</p>

<h3>What Damages Can You Recover?</h3>

<p>In a Michigan car accident case, you may be entitled to:</p>

  <li>Pain and suffering compensation (in third-party claims)</li>
  <li>Excess economic damages beyond PIP limits</li>
  <li>Mini-tort claims for vehicle damage (up to $3,000)</li>
</ul>

<h3>2024-2025 Michigan Car Accident Law Updates</h3>

<p>Several recent changes affect Michigan car accident claims:</p>

<p><strong>Medical Provider Reimbursement:</strong> As of July 2024, medical providers can receive up to 240% of Medicare rates for treating auto accident patients. This increase from previous caps may improve access to care for seriously injured victims.</p>

<p><strong>Modified Comparative Negligence:</strong> Michigan follows a modified comparative fault rule. You can recover damages if you were less than 50% at fault for the accident, but your compensation is reduced by your percentage of fault. If you're found more than 50% at fault, you cannot recover non-economic damages like pain and suffering.</p>

<p><strong>Statute of Limitations:</strong> You have three years from the accident date to file a personal injury lawsuit. However, you must notify your no-fault insurance company of your claim within one year of the accident, with limited exceptions.</p>

<p><strong>Bodily Injury Liability Minimums:</strong> Since 2020, all Michigan drivers must carry bodily injury liability coverage of at least $250,000 per person and $500,000 per accident (with an option to reduce to $50,000/$100,000). This provides more coverage when pursuing claims against at-fault drivers.</p>
