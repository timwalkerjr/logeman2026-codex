<h2>Legal Duty of Property Owners in Wayne County</h2>

<p>Michigan law imposes different duties on property owners depending on why a visitor is on the property:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Invitees</strong>—customers at stores, restaurants, entertainment venues, and other businesses. Owners must inspect the premises, fix known hazards, and warn of dangers that a reasonable inspection would have discovered.</li>
  <li><strong>Licensees</strong>—social guests and others whose presence is permitted but not for business purposes. Owners must warn of known hazards but generally have no duty to inspect.</li>
  <li><strong>Trespassers</strong>—people who enter without permission. Owners generally owe only a duty to refrain from willful and wanton misconduct, except when children are attracted to dangerous artificial conditions.</li>
</ul>

<p>Most Wayne County premises liability cases involve invitees injured at commercial establishments. We know how to establish the property owner's duty and breach in these complex cases.</p>

<h2>Common Wayne County Premises Liability Hazards</h2>

<h3>Snow and Ice Hazards</h3>

<p>Wayne County winters create dangerous conditions at apartment complexes, shopping centers, gas stations, restaurants, and office buildings. Property owners who fail to clear snow, salt walkways, and address icy conditions create serious liability. Many municipalities—including Detroit—have local ordinances requiring timely snow removal, and violations can establish negligence.</p>

<h3>Wet Floors, Spills, and Trip Hazards</h3>

<p>Retail stores, grocery stores, and restaurants regularly create hazardous conditions through poor maintenance practices. Spilled liquids, recently mopped floors, torn carpeting, uneven thresholds, and cluttered aisles all cause slip-and-fall accidents that result in serious injuries.</p>

<h3>Broken Stairs, Railings, and Elevators</h3>

<p>Defective staircases, missing or broken handrails, and malfunctioning elevators cause catastrophic injuries. Property owners must maintain these features in safe operating condition and address known defects promptly.</p>

<h3>Negligent Security</h3>

<p>Apartment buildings, hotels, parking garages, and shopping centers in high-crime areas have a duty to provide reasonable security measures. Inadequate lighting, broken locks, missing security cameras, and absence of security personnel can expose property owners to liability when violent crimes occur on the premises.</p>

<h3>Dangerous Conditions at Public Venues</h3>

<p>Detroit is home to major sports venues, casinos, concert halls, and entertainment districts. When these venues have unsafe conditions—defective seating, broken railings, inadequate crowd control—injured visitors may have premises liability claims against the operators.</p>

<h3>Apartment and Rental Property Hazards</h3>

<p>Landlords in Wayne County have legal duties to maintain rental properties in reasonably safe condition. Broken stairs, defective porches, faulty electrical wiring, broken locks, and pest infestations can all form the basis of premises liability claims against landlords.</p>

<h2>The Open and Obvious Doctrine and Its Exceptions</h2>

<p>Michigan's open and obvious doctrine is a common defense in premises liability cases. Under this rule, property owners generally don't have to warn of hazards that visitors can see and understand. However, the doctrine is not absolute:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Special aspects exception.</strong> When a hazard poses an extreme risk of severe harm—regardless of whether it's open and obvious—the property owner can still be liable.</li>
  <li><strong>Distraction exception.</strong> When conditions exist that distract a reasonable person from noticing an obvious hazard, the doctrine doesn't bar recovery.</li>
  <li><strong>Effectively unavoidable condition.</strong> When visitors have no reasonable way to avoid the hazard, the condition isn't truly avoidable.</li>
  <li><strong>Reasonable foreseeability.</strong> Property owners who know that visitors will encounter a hazard despite its obviousness may still have a duty to address it.</li>
</ul>

<p>These exceptions are highly fact-specific. Our attorneys analyze every premises liability case to identify the legal theories that preserve your right to compensation.</p>

<h2>Compensation You May Recover</h2>

<p>Victims of Wayne County premises liability incidents can pursue compensation for:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Past and future medical expenses</strong>—emergency care, surgery, hospitalization, rehabilitation, physical therapy, prescription medications, and ongoing treatment</li>
  <li><strong>Lost wages</strong>—income lost during recovery</li>
  <li><strong>Diminished earning capacity</strong>—when injuries prevent you from returning to your previous work or earning the same income</li>
  <li><strong>Pain and suffering</strong>—physical pain, emotional distress, and loss of enjoyment of life</li>
  <li><strong>Permanent disability</strong>—compensation for lasting impairments</li>
  <li><strong>Out-of-pocket expenses</strong>—transportation, home modifications, and household help during recovery</li>
</ul>

<p>For severe injuries requiring lifetime care, economic damages alone can reach millions of dollars.</p>

<h2>Special Rules for Government Property Claims</h2>

<p>Many Wayne County premises liability incidents occur on government property—the Detroit Institute of Arts, city recreation centers, public libraries, county buildings, or the Detroit People Mover. Claims against government entities have unique requirements:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Strict Notice of Claim deadlines—often 60 to 120 days</li>
  <li>The governmental immunity act limits liability in many circumstances</li>
  <li>Damage caps may apply</li>
  <li>Specific court procedures must be followed</li>
</ul>

<p>Missing these deadlines permanently bars your claim. Our attorneys understand these procedural requirements and ensure all deadlines are met.</p>

<h2>What to Do After a Wayne County Premises Injury</h2>

<p>Taking the right steps immediately after an incident protects your health and strengthens your legal claim:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Report the incident</strong> to the property owner, manager, or appropriate employee. Request a copy of any incident report.</li>
  <li><strong>Document everything</strong>—take photos and videos of the hazard, your injuries, lighting conditions, and surrounding area before conditions change.</li>
  <li><strong>Get witness information</strong>—collect names and contact information from anyone who saw your fall.</li>
  <li><strong>Seek medical attention immediately</strong>—some injuries like concussions and internal injuries may not be apparent right away. Prompt medical care also creates documentation of your injuries.</li>
  <li><strong>Preserve physical evidence</strong>—keep the clothing and shoes you were wearing.</li>
  <li><strong>Don't give recorded statements</strong> to insurance adjusters without speaking with an attorney first.</li>
  <li><strong>Contact an experienced premises liability attorney</strong> as soon as possible.</li>
</ol>
